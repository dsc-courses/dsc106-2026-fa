Designing a visualization and critiquing one are the same skill pointed in
opposite directions. In Project 2 you made deliberate choices about
transformation, encoding, color, and framing, and you had to justify each of
them. Here you will reconstruct someone else's reasoning from the finished
artifact alone: naming the marks and channels they chose, judging whether those
choices worked, and explaining why — all in three minutes.

## Your Tasks

1. **Find a visualization.** As a team, select at least one interesting data
   visualization that has **not** already been shown in lecture, labs, or a
   project. It can come from a news outlet, a scientific paper, a dashboard, a
   government report, a social media post, a company's marketing page —
   anywhere. It does not have to be good; a "not-so-great" visualization often
   makes for the most instructive critique. If the dataset behind the
   visualization is publicly available, find it and include a link.

2. **Analyze it** using concepts from Lectures 1–6: the value of visualization,
   data and image models, visual encoding (expressiveness and effectiveness),
   dark patterns and deception, perception, and color. Your analysis must cover:

   - **(a) Marks and channels.** Identify the marks and the visual channels used
     to encode each data attribute, and the data type of each attribute
     (nominal, ordinal, quantitative).
   - **(b) Strengths.** What does this visualization do well, and why? Tie each
     strength to a specific design choice and to a lecture concept — "the
     diverging color scale makes the zero crossing legible at a glance," not
     "the colors are nice."
   - **(c) Weaknesses.** What does it do poorly, and why? Tie each weakness to a
     lecture concept, explain what it costs the reader, and, where you can,
     suggest what you would change.

3. **Prepare your elevator pitch.** Build a single slide containing the
   visualization, and annotate the visualization directly (see
   [The Elevator Pitch](#the-elevator-pitch)).

4. **Submit** your slide and links to Gradescope before lecture on Thursday
   10/29, then **present** at the Project 2.5 Showcase.

5. **Peer review** other teams' pitches individually (see
   [Peer Review](#peer-review)).

## The Elevator Pitch

Your team will deliver a **3-minute** pitch of your critique at the showcase.

- **3 minutes, hard stop.** We will keep time, and you will be cut off at 3:00 so
  that every team gets to present.
- **One slide: the visualization itself.** No bullet-point or text-only slides.
  The only additions allowed are **annotations on the visualization** — arrows,
  circles, highlights, and short labels that point at the marks, channels,
  strengths, and weaknesses you are talking about.

Practice out loud and time yourselves. Three minutes goes quickly; decide in
advance which one or two points matter most for each of strengths and
weaknesses.

### Peer Review

This part is also done in **groups**. You will finish this part, with your team, in class.

During the showcase, the professor will queue the class that the following next three pitches for each of you is what you need to peer-review. For
each, give constructive feedback on their pitch using the
["I like / I wish / What if?"][ilike] format: positive feedback on effective
aspects, critical (but respectful!) feedback on what could be improved, and
more speculative ideas they could have explored. Consider:

- Did they identify the marks and channels correctly and completely?
- Were the strengths and weaknesses tied to specific design choices and to
  concepts from lecture, or did they stay general?
- Did their critique change how you read the visualization?
- Were the annotations on the slide clear and helpful?

For **each** of the three teams, write **at least two** statements in **each**
of the I like / I wish / What if? categories — at least 6 statements per team
and at least 18 in total. Submit on Google Form during class.

[ilike]: https://github.com/dsc-courses/dsc106-wi24/raw/gh-pages/resources/reading/I-Like-I-Wish-What-If.pdf

## Grading

The team portion is scored out of a maximum of 14 points, and the peer review
out of 1 point.

{:#rubric.rubric}
| Component | Excellent | Satisfactory | Poor |
| --------- | --------- | ------------ | ---- |
| **Marks and Channels** | Marks and channels are identified correctly and completely, with the data type each channel encodes. Dataset is linked where one is public.<br>_(3 points)_ | Marks and channels are mostly correct, but some encodings are missed or mislabeled.<br>_(2 points)_ | Marks and channels are missing or largely incorrect.<br>_(1 point)_ |
| **Strengths** | Identifies real strengths and ties each to a concrete design choice and a named concept from Lectures 1–6.<br>_(4 points)_ | Identifies real strengths, but the reasoning stays general ("it's clear," "the colors work") or the lecture connection is surface-level.<br>_(3 points)_ | Strengths are asserted without support, or are not actually strengths.<br>_(1.5 points)_ |
| **Weaknesses** | Identifies substantive weaknesses, ties each to a concept from Lectures 1–6, explains the cost to the reader, and proposes plausible alternatives.<br>_(4 points)_ | Identifies real weaknesses, but stops short of explaining the cost or suggesting a fix.<br>_(3 points)_ | Weaknesses are nitpicks, or the critique is uncritical.<br>_(1.5 points)_ |
| **Elevator Pitch** | Pitch finishes within 3 minutes and covers all required content. The slide is the visualization with clear annotations that support what is being said.<br>_(3 points)_ | Pitch runs over or skips required content, or the annotations add little.<br>_(2 points)_ | Pitch is missing, or the slide is not the annotated visualization.<br>_(0 points)_ |
| **Originality** | You found something nobody else would have, or read it in a way the rest of us hadn't considered.<br>_(up to +1 bonus point)_ | You met all the parameters of the assignment.<br>_(0 points)_ | – |

**Peer review (1 point, group):** full credit for three complete reviews
that meet the minimum number of statements and are specific to each pitch.

## Where to Look

You are free to find a visualization anywhere. If you want starting points:

- [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/) — visual essays, frequently experimental.
- [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/) — charts with the underlying data published alongside.
- [Reuters Graphics](https://www.reuters.com/graphics/) and [The New York Times' The Upshot](https://www.nytimes.com/section/upshot) — news visualization.
- [r/dataisbeautiful](https://www.reddit.com/r/dataisbeautiful/) and [r/dataisugly](https://www.reddit.com/r/dataisugly/) — a wide quality range, which is useful here.
- [Information is Beautiful Awards](https://www.informationisbeautifulawards.com/) — award winners and shortlists.
- The dashboard, report, or chart your internship, research lab, or favorite hobby site put in front of you last week.

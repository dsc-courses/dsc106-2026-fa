Designing a visualization and critiquing one are the same skill pointed in
opposite directions. In Project 2 you made deliberate choices about
transformation, encoding, color, and framing, and you had to justify each of
them. Here you will reconstruct someone else's reasoning from the finished
artifact alone — naming what they chose, guessing why, and judging whether it
worked.

## Your Tasks

1. **Find a visualization.** As a team, find one interesting data visualization
   that has **not** already been shown in lecture, labs, or a project. It can
   come from a news outlet, a scientific paper, a dashboard, a government
   report, a social media post, a company's marketing page — anywhere. It does
   not have to be good. Some of the most instructive critiques are of
   visualizations that fail.

2. **Write your critique** as three paragraphs of roughly 3–4 sentences each:

   - **(a) Summary and connection.** Summarize what the visualization shows and
     describe how it connects to a concept from lecture — visual encoding,
     expressiveness and effectiveness, perception, color, interaction, or
     persuasion and deception. Name the concept explicitly. If the underlying
     dataset is publicly available, include a link to it.

   - **(b) Strengths.** Describe what this particular visualization does well,
     and why. Be specific about the design choices responsible — "the diverging
     color scale makes the zero crossing legible at a glance" rather than "the
     colors are nice."

   - **(c) Weaknesses.** Describe what this particular visualization does poorly,
     and why. Where possible, say what you would change and what you would
     expect that change to buy you.

3. **Post it to Ed** under the **Critiques** category. Include the
   visualization itself (screenshot or embed), a link to the original source,
   the dataset link if one exists, your three paragraphs, and the full names of
   every team member.

4. **Prepare for the showcase.** Bring a single slide — the visualization plus
   three or four bullets — that your team can walk through in about two minutes
   during the Project 2.5 Showcase on Thursday 10/29.

## The Showcase

On **Thursday, October 29**, we will spend lecture on your critiques. Come
having read at least three other teams' posts. We will hear a selection of
teams present their visualization and critique, discuss them as a class, and
vote on a few categories — most surprising, most deceptive, best redesign
suggestion.

Presenting is not separately graded beyond the showcase slide; the point of the
session is to see how differently ten teams can read the same design vocabulary.

## Grading

The assignment score is out of a maximum of 14 points.

{:#rubric.rubric}
| Component | Excellent | Satisfactory | Poor |
| --------- | --------- | ------------ | ---- |
| **Summary and Lecture Connection** | The visualization is summarized accurately and economically. A specific lecture concept is named and genuinely applied, not just mentioned. Dataset is linked where one is public.<br>_(4 points)_ | The visualization is summarized and a lecture concept is named, but the connection stays surface-level.<br>_(3 points)_ | Summary is inaccurate or vague, or no lecture concept is identified.<br>_(1.5 points)_ |
| **Strengths** | Identifies non-obvious strengths and ties each to a concrete design choice and its perceptual or rhetorical effect.<br>_(4 points)_ | Identifies real strengths, but the reasoning stays general ("it's clear," "the colors work").<br>_(3 points)_ | Strengths are asserted without support, or are not actually strengths.<br>_(1.5 points)_ |
| **Weaknesses** | Identifies substantive weaknesses, explains why each one hurts the reader, and proposes plausible alternatives.<br>_(4 points)_ | Identifies real weaknesses, but stops short of explaining the cost or suggesting a fix.<br>_(3 points)_ | Weaknesses are nitpicks, or the critique is uncritical.<br>_(1.5 points)_ |
| **Showcase Slide and Participation** | Slide is clear and self-contained; team came prepared having read other teams' posts.<br>_(2 points)_ | Slide is present but thin.<br>_(1 point)_ | Missing.<br>_(0 points)_ |
| **Originality** | You found something nobody else would have, or read it in a way the rest of us hadn't considered.<br>_(up to +1 bonus point)_ | You met all the parameters of the assignment.<br>_(0 points)_ | – |

## Where to Look

You are free to find a visualization anywhere. If you want starting points:

- [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/) — visual essays, frequently experimental.
- [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/) — charts with the underlying data published alongside.
- [Reuters Graphics](https://www.reuters.com/graphics/) and [The New York Times' The Upshot](https://www.nytimes.com/section/upshot) — news visualization.
- [r/dataisbeautiful](https://www.reddit.com/r/dataisbeautiful/) and [r/dataisugly](https://www.reddit.com/r/dataisugly/) — a wide quality range, which is useful here.
- [Information is Beautiful Awards](https://www.informationisbeautifulawards.com/) — award winners and shortlists.
- The dashboard, report, or chart your internship, research lab, or favorite hobby site put in front of you last week.
